Model Internals · Part 02

J-space and Silent Reasoning

Is there a small internal blackboard where a few concepts become temporarily available—readable through language and usable by downstream computation? J-space offers a testable version of that idea, but it covers only a small fraction of flexible, reportable processing.

Paper: Anthropic / Transformer CircuitsTerms: Jacobian lens · sparse subframe · causal swap

The short version. J-space is not a fixed layer, a newly discovered attention head, or another name for the residual stream. At each position it is a sparse subframe spanned by a few token-linked J-lens vectors. It typically explains less than 10% of activation variance, yet tracks concepts involved in flexible, reportable computation.

Logit lens reads output; J-lens reads influence

A logit lens sends an intermediate residual activation directly through the unembedding and asks, “Which token would this resemble if decoded now?” But later nonlinear computation has not happened yet. An intermediate activation's influence on the final output need not travel along the direct unembedding direction.

The Jacobian lens asks a more causal question: if we nudge an activation at an intermediate position, how will the final residual state or logits change? The researchers average the Jacobian of the remaining network and map every vocabulary output direction back to that layer. A direction labeled “France” does not mean a neuron literally is France; it means movement along that direction predictably shifts the final output in a France-like way.

J-space is a dynamic coordinate system, not a fixed brain region

At each token position, non-negative sparse reconstruction selects a small number of J-lens vectors to form J-space; experiments commonly cap sparsity at k ≤ 25. It is better understood as a concept coordinate system assembled for the current computation. Its contents vary with prompt, position, and layer. Individual directions are vocabulary-interpretable, while their combination can still be a bag of concepts without complete syntax or relational structure.

This is the precise sense in which reasoning may be silent. A concept can fail to appear in visible chain of thought yet be readable with J-lens and causally affect later computation. “Silent” means unexpressed—not a mystical inner monologue, and not a claim that the method recovers every private thought.

Five tests move from interpretation toward workspace

PropertyExperimental questionObservation
Verbal reportCan the model report the internal concept?J-lens concepts correspond to later explanations.
Directed modulationWhat happens when a concept direction is written?Related answers increase selectively rather than through random disruption.
Internal reasoningDoes it participate before output?Ablation or replacement changes downstream answers.
Flexible generalizationDo associated facts update when a concept changes?France→China shifts downstream facts toward China.
SelectivityDoes all computation pass through it?No. Much automatic processing bypasses J-space.

The clearest counterfactual is a concept swap. Replacing “spider” with “ant” internally moves a downstream answer from eight legs to six. Replacing France with China updates related facts such as the capital rather than merely repeating a new country name. The intervention changes a variable consumed by later computation, not just the final word.

Its most informative result is that it does not do everything

Broad J-space ablation preserves fluent language and many basic capabilities while substantially hurting complex, flexible reasoning. That selectivity is more informative than saying all activations matter. J-space looks like an on-demand broadcast workspace; practiced, local, and automatic computation can use other routes.

The limits matter. Single-token names are easier to read than multiword entities. Relations are difficult. Readout methods sometimes disagree. The boundary between workspace and motor/output systems remains unclear, and we do not know which tasks must route through it. Highly practiced behavior may rely on it less. The evidence therefore supports a workspace-like mechanism; it neither establishes a complete Global Workspace Theory implementation nor demonstrates phenomenal consciousness.

Its relationship to agents, memory, and self-evolution

For agents: an internal bus in the decision chain

J-space may carry goals, entities, constraints, or role perspectives that several downstream computations need before a tool call is emitted. Whether that call executes still belongs to the agent runtime. The mechanism can explain part of why a model favored an action; it is not an authorization system.

For memory: a working set, not a storage layer

A J-space activation disappears with the forward pass. External memory can affect it only after retrieval and context injection. In operating-system terms, persistent memory resembles disk, context resembles loaded pages, and J-space resembles a small working area shared across computations.

For self-evolution: training changes default perspective and routing

The paper finds that post-training moves J-space toward an Assistant perspective. Reflection training can then alter behavior through ethics- and reflection-related directions in the workspace. Training changes more than stored knowledge: it can change which concepts are broadcast and how they enter decisions. Part five separates this weight-level change from edits to external skills and memories.

How to use J-space as an instrument

A credible workflow does not end with a screenshot labeled “the model thought X.” First read candidate concepts during natural tasks. Cross-check them with another method. Then write, swap, or ablate the direction. Finally test whether the behavioral change is specific and replicates across prompts. A readout without a counterfactual is a lead; an intervention that destroys general fluency may still fail to identify a specific mechanism.
